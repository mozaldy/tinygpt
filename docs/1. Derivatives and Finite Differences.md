# Derivatives and Finite Differences

> [!summary] Key idea
> A **derivative** measures how quickly a function changes at a particular point. In machine learning, the derivative of the **loss** with respect to a parameter tells us which direction changing that parameter will move the loss. We can calculate derivatives **analytically** using algebra/calculus, or **approximate** them using finite differences.

> [!info] Study record
> Reconstructed from our September 2026 TinyGPT exercises, rather than transcribed from the original Excalidraw canvas. The examples and results below are the ones we worked through together.

## 1. What a loss function represents

Imagine a model with a single adjustable parameter, $w$. For this exercise our loss function is:

$$
L(w)=(w-3)^2
$$

- $w$ is the parameter we can change.
- $L(w)$ measures how far $w$ is from the target value $3$, squared.
- A smaller loss is better.
- The smallest possible loss is $0$, achieved at $w=3$.

| $w$ | $L(w)$ |
|---:|---:|
| 0 | 9 |
| 2 | 1 |
| 3 | 0 |
| 4 | 1 |
| 5 | 4 |

This loss forms a **U-shaped parabola**. Moving toward $w=3$ lowers the loss; moving away raises it.

> [!question] Intuition check
> If the loss at $w=2$ is $1$, would increasing $w$ slightly make the loss larger or smaller? What about increasing $w$ slightly when $w=4$?

## 2. What a derivative means

The notation

$$
\frac{dL}{dw}
$$

is read as *the derivative of $L$ with respect to $w$*. It means: how fast does the loss change when we make a small change to $w$?

A derivative is the **slope at the current point**, not the loss value itself.

- **Negative derivative:** increasing $w$ a little tends to **decrease** the loss.
- **Positive derivative:** increasing $w$ a little tends to **increase** the loss.
- **Zero derivative:** the function is locally flat; this does not always guarantee a minimum for arbitrary functions.

For this particular parabola, the slope is negative to the left of $3$, zero at $3$, and positive to the right.

### Why the notation looks like a fraction

In $dy/dx$, $y$ is the changing output and $x$ is the input variable. The derivative asks how $y$ responds to a very small change in $x$. Strictly speaking, a derivative is defined as a **limit** of ratios of changes.

$$
\frac{dL}{dw}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{L(w+h)-L(w)}{h}
$$

Here, $h$ is a small step in $w$.

## 3. Calculating the derivative analytically

Start with:

$$
L(w)=(w-3)^2
$$

Expand the square:

$$
L(w)=w^2-6w+9
$$

Use three differentiation rules:

| Expression | Derivative with respect to $w$ |
|---|---|
| $w^n$ | $nw^{n-1}$ (power rule) |
| $cw$ | $c$ for constant $c$ |
| constant $c$ | $0$ |

Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dL}{dw}
&=\frac{d}{dw}(w^2-6w+9)\\
&=2w-6\\
&=\boxed{2(w-3)}
\end{aligned}
$$

### Evaluate the slope at different points

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left.\frac{dL}{dw}\right|_{w=0}&=2(0-3)=\boxed{-6}\\
\left.\frac{dL}{dw}\right|_{w=3}&=2(3-3)=\boxed{0}\\
\left.\frac{dL}{dw}\right|_{w=5}&=2(5-3)=\boxed{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

At $w=0$, the derivative is $-6$: moving $w$ upward slightly lowers the loss. At $w=5$, the derivative is $4$: moving $w$ downward slightly lowers the loss.

> [!note] Why does the power rule produce $2w$?
> For $w^2$, the derivative is $2w^{2-1}=2w$. The exponent moves in front and decreases by one. Later, the chain rule will let us differentiate expressions such as $(w-3)^2$ *without* expanding them first.

## 4. Approximating the derivative with finite differences

What if we want an estimate of a slope from function evaluations, without first deriving its formula?

Use the **forward finite difference**:

$$
\frac{dL}{dw}\approx\frac{L(w+h)-L(w)}{h}
$$

At $w=0$:

$$
L(0)=(-3)^2=9
$$

And:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{L(0+h)-L(0)}{h}
&=\frac{(h-3)^2-9}{h}\\
&=\frac{h^2-6h}{h}\\
&=-6+h
\end{aligned}
$$

Try the three step sizes from our exercise:

| Step size $h$ | Finite-difference estimate |
|---:|---:|
| $0.01$ | $-5.99$ |
| $0.001$ | $-5.999$ |
| $0.0001$ | $-5.9999$ |
| Analytical derivative | **$-6$** |

As $h$ gets smaller, the estimate approaches the exact derivative at $w=0$.

> [!important] Very small is not always better on a computer
> For a mathematically exact function, shrinking $h$ makes this forward-difference estimate approach the derivative. On a computer, *extremely* small $h$ can introduce floating-point rounding error.

## 5. Analytical vs. numerical derivatives

| Analytical differentiation | Numerical differentiation |
|---|---|
| Uses calculus to find a derivative expression | Estimates a derivative from nearby function values |
| Gives $2(w-3)$ in this example | Gives values such as $-5.9999$ at $w=0$ |
| Can then be evaluated at any $w$ | Depends on the chosen point and step size $h$ |
| The approach we later automate with backpropagation | Useful for intuition and for checking gradients |

Do not confuse **evaluating an analytical derivative at a number** with **numerical differentiation**. For example, calculating $2(0-3)=-6$ is still analytical differentiation followed by evaluation.

## 6. How this connects to training a neural network

In this toy example, there is only one parameter, $w$. The derivative tells us how a change in that parameter affects loss. Training involves using that information to move $w$ toward values with lower loss.

Our next topic adds an **update rule** to actually change the parameter:

$$
w_{\text{new}}=w-\eta\frac{dL}{dw}
$$

We will learn exactly why this works in [[2. Gradient Descent and Partial Derivatives]].
