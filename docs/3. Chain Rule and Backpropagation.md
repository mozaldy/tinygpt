# Chain Rule and Backpropagation

> [!summary] Key idea
> **The chain rule** tells us how a change in one variable affects a final result through intermediate operations. **Backpropagation** applies the chain rule backward through a computational graph to find gradients efficiently. **Gradient descent** uses those gradients to update the model's parameters and reduce its loss.

## 1. Why the chain rule matters

A neural network computes its output in stages. The loss depends on the output; the output depends on intermediate calculations; and those calculations depend on the model's weights and biases.

To train the model, we need to know **how much the loss changes when a parameter changes**. The chain rule connects the derivatives of each stage so we can calculate that effect.

For example, if the loss $L$ depends on $c$, $c$ depends on $a$, and $a$ depends on $x$, then:

$$
\frac{dL}{dx}=\frac{dL}{dc}\cdot\frac{dc}{da}\cdot\frac{da}{dx}
$$

Think of this as tracing how a change in $x$ propagates through every operation until it affects the loss.

## 2. Worked example: a chain of operations

We will use $c$ for the second intermediate value. This keeps $b$ available later as the standard symbol for a **bias**.
![[Pasted image 20261017120058.png]]
$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= 2 \\
a &= 3x = 6 \\
c &= a + 4 = 10 \\
L &= c^2 = 100
\end{aligned}
$$

### Step 1 — Forward pass

Calculate the intermediate values in order, starting from $x$ and ending at the loss $L$.

![[mermaid-diagram.png]]

The **forward pass** computes the result of each operation and ultimately the loss.

### Step 2 — Local derivatives

Differentiate each individual operation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{da}{dx} &= 3 \\
\frac{dc}{da} &= 1 \\
\frac{dL}{dc} &= 2c = 20
\end{aligned}
$$

Each is a **local derivative**: it describes how the output of one operation changes with respect to its immediate input.

### Step 3 — Apply the chain rule

To find how the loss changes with respect to the original input $x$, multiply the derivatives along the path:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dL}{dx}
&= \frac{dL}{dc}\cdot\frac{dc}{da}\cdot\frac{da}{dx} \\
&= 20\cdot 1\cdot 3 \\
&= \boxed{60}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Interpretation:** at $x=2$, a small increase in $x$ produces a change in $L$ of approximately $60$ times that increase. This is a *local* rate of change, not a claim that the slope is always $60$.

**Sanity check:** substitute the intermediate expressions directly:

$$
L=(3x+4)^2
$$

Differentiating gives:

$$
\frac{dL}{dx}=2(3x+4)\cdot 3=6(3x+4)
$$

At $x=2$, this is $6(10)=60$, matching our chain-rule calculation.

## 3. Chain rule vs. backpropagation vs. gradient descent

| Concept | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Chain rule** | Mathematical rule for derivatives of composite functions. |
| **Backpropagation** | Efficiently computes gradients by applying the chain rule backward through the computational graph. |
| **Gradient descent** | Uses those gradients to update parameters in the direction that locally reduces the loss. |

Backpropagation does **not** update the weights by itself. It finds the gradients; the optimizer performs the updates.

For an intermediate value $a$, for example:

$$
\frac{dL}{dx}=\frac{dL}{da}\frac{da}{dx}
$$

If $a$ also depends on a weight $w$, then:

$$
\frac{dL}{dw}=\frac{dL}{da}\frac{da}{dw}
$$

The gradient arriving from later computations, $\frac{dL}{da}$, gets multiplied by each operation's local derivative as we move backward.

## 4. Calculating weight and bias gradients

Consider a simple model:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a &= wx+b \\
L &= a^2
\end{aligned}
$$

Here:

- $x$ is the input, treated as fixed during this parameter update.
- $w$ is the **weight** (trainable parameter).
- $b$ is the **bias** (trainable parameter).
- $a$ is the model's output in this simplified example.
- $L$ is the loss we want to minimize.

### Weight gradient

First find the derivative of the loss with respect to $a$, and the derivative of $a$ with respect to $w$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dL}{da} &= 2a \\
\frac{\partial a}{\partial w} &= x
\end{aligned}
$$

Then apply the chain rule:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}
&=\frac{dL}{da}\cdot\frac{\partial a}{\partial w} \\
&=2a\cdot x \\
&=\boxed{2(wx+b)x}
\end{aligned}
$$

### Bias gradient

The derivative of $a=wx+b$ with respect to $b$ is $1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial a}{\partial b}&=1 \\
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}
&=\frac{dL}{da}\cdot\frac{\partial a}{\partial b} \\
&=2a\cdot 1 \\
&=\boxed{2(wx+b)}
\end{aligned}
$$

> [!note] Notation
> We use **partial derivatives** ($\partial$) for $w$ and $b$ because the loss depends on more than one parameter. When evaluating a partial derivative, hold the other independent variables constant.

## 5. One complete gradient descent training step

Given:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x&=2 \\
w&=3 \\
b&=1 \\
\eta&=0.01
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\eta$ (eta) is the **learning rate**.

Our model and loss are:

$$
a=wx+b,\qquad L=a^2
$$

### Step 1 — Forward pass

$$
\begin{aligned}
a&=3\cdot2+1=\boxed{7} \\
L&=7^2=\boxed{49}
\end{aligned}
$$

### Step 2 — Backward pass: calculate gradients

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}
&=2(wx+b)x \\
&=2(7)(2) \\
&=\boxed{28} \\
\\
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}
&=2(wx+b) \\
&=2(7) \\
&=\boxed{14}
\end{aligned}
$$

These gradients are **derived analytically and then evaluated numerically**. This is different from *numerical differentiation*, which approximates a derivative using methods such as finite differences.

### Step 3 — Update parameters

Gradient descent subtracts a learning-rate-scaled gradient from each parameter:

$$
\begin{aligned}
w_{\text{new}}
&=w-\eta\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} \\
&=3-0.01(28) \\
&=\boxed{2.72} \\
\\
b_{\text{new}}
&=b-\eta\frac{\partial L}{\partial b} \\
&=1-0.01(14) \\
&=\boxed{0.86}
\end{aligned}
$$

### Step 4 — Check the new loss

Run the forward pass again using the **updated** parameters:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_{\text{new}}
&=2.72(2)+0.86 \\
&=6.30 \\
L_{\text{new}}
&=6.30^2 \\
&=\boxed{39.69}
\end{aligned}
$$

The loss decreased from **49** to **39.69**, a reduction of **9.31** in this step.

> [!important] Lower loss does not happen automatically for every step
> A learning rate that is too large can make the loss increase or cause optimization to oscillate. In this example, the selected learning rate reduces the loss.

## 6. Why gradients must be recalculated

A gradient is the slope of the loss function **at the current parameter values**. When $w$ and $b$ change, we are at a different point, so the gradients may change too.

Our original gradients were:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}=28,\qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}=14
$$

After updating the parameters, $a=6.3$, so the next gradients are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}&=2(6.3)(2)=\boxed{25.2} \\
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}&=2(6.3)=\boxed{12.6}
\end{aligned}
$$

They are different from the first gradients. So a new iteration must recompute the forward pass and backward pass **before** updating again.

## 7. The four steps of a training loop

1. **Forward pass:** Calculate the model output and its loss.
2. **Backward pass:** Apply the chain rule to calculate gradients of the loss with respect to the parameters.
3. **Parameter update:** Use an optimizer (such as gradient descent) to adjust the parameters.
4. **Repeat:** Compute a new output, loss, and gradients with the updated parameters.

A useful shorthand:

$$
\text{Forward} \rightarrow \text{Loss} \rightarrow \text{Backward} \rightarrow \text{Update} \rightarrow \text{Repeat}
$$
